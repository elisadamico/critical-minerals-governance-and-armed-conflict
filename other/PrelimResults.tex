\section{Potential Explanation}

Africa sits at the center of the global energy transition. As demand surges for critical minerals (from lithium for batteries to gallium for semiconductors) a key question emerges: do these ``technology minerals'' reshape conflict dynamics differently than traditional extractives like gold or diamonds?

Our analysis of mineral exploration and conflict across Africa (1995--2018) at seven spatial scales (25--300km) reveals heterogeneous effects. Critical minerals diverge based on governance: regulated minerals (Tin, Tantalum) and industrial-only minerals (Molybdenum, PGMs) reduce both conflict frequency and lethality. But ungoverned strategic minerals (Gallium, Germanium, Zinc, Chromium) exhibit fewer conflicts but \textit{higher} deaths per event. These minerals reduce opportunistic violence through formalization, but their high strategic value without governance frameworks attracts state militaries and professional armed groups. When violence occurs, it is deadlier.

\subsection{The Governance-Value Nexus}

We identify three patterns: (1) \textbf{Governed Critical} minerals (Tin, Tantalum, Molybdenum, Copper, Nickel, PGMs) pacify through international frameworks or industrial barriers. (2) \textbf{Ungoverned Strategic} minerals (Gallium, Germanium, Zinc, Chromium) where high geopolitical value without governance creates fewer but deadlier conflicts. (3) \textbf{Traditional Extractives} (Coal, Diamond, Gold) with weak governance continue resource curse patterns. 

Africa provides the a test case: if minerals can pacify here, they can pacify anywhere. The Dodd-Frank success with 3T minerals proves governance works in Africa; our findings on gallium and germanium reveal urgent governance gaps for semiconductor minerals.

\subsection{Key Robustness Findings}

\subsubsection{1. Gallium \& Germanium: Perfect Twin Replication}
\begin{itemize}
\item Systematic decoupling: reduce events, increase deaths
\item Effect strengthens 3$\times$ from coarse to fine resolution (3$\rightarrow$9 periods)
\item Both are semiconductor minerals
\item Identical patterns across all 7 spatial scales
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{2. Iron: Strongest Overall Effect}
\begin{itemize}
\item Non-critical but massive pacification (up to 21 periods)
\item NO lethality increase (unlike critical minerals)
\item Validates `formalization without strategic competition' theory
\item Effect strengthens monotonically with finer resolution
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Molybdenum: Most Consistent Critical Mineral}
\begin{itemize}
\item Present at ALL scales tested
\item Often 0 pre-trends (strong causal identification)
\item Pure pacification effect without lethality increase
\item Effect strengthens from 3$\rightarrow$9 periods
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4. PGMs: Stable Across Scales}
\begin{itemize}
\item Consistently reduces non-state violence (8--11 periods)
\item Ultra-rare $\rightarrow$ state monopoly control hypothesis
\item Effect size remarkably stable across all scales
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{5. Coal: Exception That Proves the Rule}
\begin{itemize}
\item ONLY mineral consistently increasing battle deaths (5--8 periods positive)
\item Low governance, artisanal extraction
\item Reduces events but increases lethality
\item Validates that strategic value + governance = pacification
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{6. Spatial Scale Matters}
\begin{itemize}
\item Technology minerals (Ga, Ge, Mo): Strongest at 25--50km
\item Traditional extractives (Gold, Iron): Strongest at 100--150km
\item Effects are NOT aggregation artifacts
\item Finer resolution reveals micro-level mechanisms
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{7. Pre-Trend Validation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Most robust minerals show $\leq$5 pre-trends
\item Molybdenum often 0 pre-trends
\item Strong causal identification across specifications
\item Passes parallel trends assumption
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Implications}

\subsection{The Technology Minerals Effect}
Critical minerals for emerging technologies (semiconductors, batteries, renewables) create a new pattern: \textbf{fewer conflicts, but more lethal when they occur}. This ``professionalization of resource violence'' distinguishes technology minerals from traditional extractives.

\subsection{The Iron Paradox}
Iron, despite being non-critical, shows the \textit{strongest} pacification effects. This validates the ``formalization without strategic competition'' hypothesis: massive industrialization reduces conflict without attracting geopolitical competition that increases lethality.

\subsection{The Governance Hypothesis}
Coal's unique pattern (only mineral increasing deaths) validates that international governance and strategic value determine conflict outcomes. Minerals with \textbf{high strategic value + strong governance} (like Tin/Tantalum under Dodd-Frank) show pure pacification. Minerals with \textbf{low value + weak governance} (like Coal) show violence increases.
